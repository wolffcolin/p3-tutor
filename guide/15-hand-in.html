Part III · The ladder

Hand in

The last stage of Project 3: the video, the check that confirms your repo holds everything, the one Canvas post, the deadline, and how I plan to grade the 150 points.

About 13 minutes to read.

The short path

Why

The last check confirms, from your committed files, that everything I grade from is in your repo, and the video is the one part of P3 that has to be you: your voice, your reasons and your story.

Do
  1. Record a 5 to 10 minute video, with your phone or a screen recorder; no Claude Code window is needed.
  2. In a Plain terminal, commit everything, run uv run p3 check --final, push, and wait for the p3-check run on GitHub to go green.
  3. On Canvas, make one post in the Project 3 discussion, with your repo's URL and your video.
Done when

uv run p3 check --final ends with Integrity: no failures. and Hand-in: N of N items done, ready to hand in., the Actions tab shows a green p3-check run on your last commit, and your post is in the discussion before Tuesday, November 24, at 11:59 pm.

Then

You are done with P3. If p3 check --final fails, When it breaks has the usual causes, and the Guide can help you read the FAIL lines.

What you hand in

You hand in three things, and they all go in one Canvas post.

  1. A public GitHub repo made from the template, named p3-tutor, whose URL you post.
  2. The work inside it: your tutor in tutor/ with its skills and subagents, every snapshot of the ladder in configs/, your runs in bench/runs/ and your blind comparisons in bench/compare/, your two test topics in bench/own/, your reviews and the component inventory in reviews/, and EVAL.md in your own words, plus PREREG.md for 598E or the length-study stretch.
  3. A 5 to 10 minute video.

When you reach this stage, uv run p3 next shows Now: 12 Hand in, and once everything p3 check --final can see is done, its Next line says that the video, the push and the Canvas post remain. Those three are the parts no command in the repo can check for you, which is why this page is about them.

The video

The video is worth 50 points, a quarter of the 200 on Canvas. Record 5 to 10 minutes, with any tool and at any quality: a phone, or a screen recording with your voice over it, is fine. I care about the story, not about production quality, so do not spend time on editing, titles or music.

What to cover

Cover three things, in whatever order feels natural to you.

  1. The project. What you built and what you found. Show your tutor's architecture (the diagram of final in section 5 of EVAL.md is a good thing to have on screen), one review that changed your tutor, the reveal of your blind comparison, and one cost table from p3 report. Then say what they do and do not let you claim: three runs of each config against each other, one person's blind picks, a judge you checked on ten labels.

  2. Your observations. Which machinery paid for itself and which did not, what surprised you, whether your blind picks agreed with your open reviews, and what your three real learning sessions taught you that the bench could not.

  3. The challenges, and how you solved them. A real one, in your words. A review-to-change story is a good one to tell: something you noticed in a round, the change it led to, and whether the next run showed that it helped. A time you overruled the agent is another.

Example: a review-to-change story, in a student's words

"In Round 2 of my c2-review file I noticed that q4 asked for the best design without saying best for what. I asked the Builder to give the quiz reviewer a check for an unstated criterion in every stem. In the next run the reviewer flagged two stems and the quiz skill rewrote them, so that change worked. But it cost about half a million tokens per quiz, and in the blind comparison I could not tell the quizzes apart, so I am not sure it was worth it."

A story like that shows the trail from a review to a change to whether the change helped, which is what the whole project is about, and it can admit that the result was mixed. A step that made things worse, or did not pay, makes as good a story as one that won.

Why I ask for a video

The skills, the subagents and the runs can come from an agent, and the video cannot. It is the one place where I hear, in your own voice, why you made the choices you made and what you think the numbers mean. That is also why you should not have an agent write a script for you to read out: a read-out script sounds like what it is, and it would put an agent's words where I asked for yours. Notes to glance at are fine.

Getting ready to record

Open what you want to show before you start, so you are not searching for files on camera: the diagram in EVAL.md, the review file with the round you will talk about, the output of uv run p3 compare --reveal <session-id> for one of your sessions, and a table from uv run p3 report. Neither command calls a model, so you can rerun them as often as you like at no cost. Then talk through it once, out loud, and keep an eye on the clock: 5 minutes is the floor and 10 the ceiling.

If you want help finding the files to show, the Guide can point you at them; picking the story and telling it are yours.

Say to the Guide
What does the video need to cover, and which files in my repo could I show for each part?

The Guide reads your repo and names files and sections; it will not write what you say.

The last check

uv run p3 check --final runs every check that uv run p3 check runs, and then it fails on anything the hand-in still needs, where plain p3 check lists the same items as TODO lines and passes. So this is the command that tells you whether your repo is complete. It reads only your files and git's history, makes no model call and costs nothing, so run it as often as you like.

Why commit before you check

Some of the checks read git's history, not just the files on your disk. Each major review must be committed complete after the run it reviews finished, each test topic must be committed before its first run, and for the length study PREREG.md must be committed before the study's first run. A review that is finished on your disk but in no commit shows up as complete here but in no commit yet; commit it. So commit first, and then check.

  1. See what is not committed yet. Read the list before you add it, because your repo is public: no API keys, passwords or private details about anyone.

    Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
    git status

  2. Commit everything you mean to hand in. That is usually configs/, bench/runs/, bench/compare/ (both files of every session), bench/own/, reviews/, tutor/, tests/ (your guard's tests), EVAL.md, PREREG.md and p3.json.

    Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
    git add -A
git commit -m "Hand in P3"

    If you would rather not type git commands, the Builder can do this for you:

    Say to the Builder
    Show me what git status lists, then commit all of it with the message "Hand in P3". Do not push yet.

  3. Run the last check.

    Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
    uv run p3 check --final

    The Guide may run this one too, since it only reads: ask it "Run p3 check --final and tell me how many items are left."

  4. Read the last two lines. They are the verdict.

    What you will see when it passes 
    Integrity: no failures.
Hand-in: N of N items done, ready to hand in.

    N depends on your section, your stretch and your test topics, so it is not the same for everyone; what matters is that both numbers are the same and the line ends with ready to hand in. The command then exits with code 0.

    If it fails

    The last line reads Hand-in: k of N items done; m still missing., and each missing item is a FAIL line above it that names the file and what is wrong. Fix them one at a time, commit, and run the check again. A review p3 check will not count and p3 check fails list the usual causes.

  5. Push, and watch CI.

    Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
    git push

    Then open the Actions tab of your repo on GitHub and wait for the p3-check run on your last commit to go green. If you have the GitHub CLI signed in, the Guide can check it for you with gh run list.

Watch out: what green does and does not mean

CI runs plain python -m p3 check on the files you pushed, not --final. A green run means nothing is broken, such as a changed fixed file or a run whose snapshot no longer matches, and it says nothing about whether you are finished. Completeness is p3 check --final, on your machine.

And p3 check --final counts what it can count, not how good it is. It cannot see your video or your Canvas post, whether your repo is public, whether your ratings are justified, or, for a stretch other than the length study, whether the stretch work is there beyond section 13 of EVAL.md.

Run it before the last evening

Run uv run p3 check --final a few days before the deadline as well as on the day, so that a FAIL line about something you forgot, such as a review committed before its run finished, has time to be fixed. On the last evening, a FAIL line that needs a new run may not be fixable at all.

The Canvas post

On Canvas you make one post in the Project 3 discussion, and it holds two things:

  • the URL of your repo, such as https://github.com/<your-github-username>/p3-tutor;
  • your video.

Run uv run p3 check --final and push your last commit before you post, so the repo I open is the repo you checked.

Before you post, check that I can see the repo: open its URL in a private browser window where you are not signed in to GitHub. If the page shows your files, it is public. If it says the page is not found, your repo is private; you can change that under the repo's Settings on GitHub.

What "measured" means

Three rows say "measured". A step is measured when its objective checks and its cost, from uv run p3 report, are set beside your major review of the step and written up in EVAL.md with what they let you claim. A table on its own is not a measurement, and neither is a review on its own: the points are for putting the two side by side and saying, honestly, what one run of a step against the step before it can and cannot show.

A step that did not pay still earns its points

A step that did not pay for itself earns its points when you measured it and said so. If your c3-split pipeline cost three times as much as c2-review and your Lesson ratings did not move, that is a real result about your system, and saying it with the numbers behind it is exactly what I am grading. Dropping that step from final for that reason is a good decision, not a lost one. As the top of EVAL.md says, what I read for above all is whether each claim matches its evidence, including the steps that did not pay.

How the reviews are graded

The reviews are 30 points, the largest single part. I look for four things in them: specific evidence, such as quotes and item ids; ratings that the written feedback justifies; feedback that someone could act on; and a visible trail from each review to the change it caused to whether that change helped. Your reviews says more, with examples.

Why an A needs a stretch

I would rather say this now than have you find out later. The core work is 125 of the 150 points, so complete core work with the video, and no stretch, is 175 of 200, which is 87.5%. So in this project an A needs a stretch. The stretch is worth doing properly and not worth doing in the last hour: pick it early, try a small piece first, and plan about 4 to 6 extra hours. Stretch options and the 598E length study describes each option.

If you are stuck for more than 20 minutes, come to office hours, Tuesday and Thursday, 9 to 10 am and 2 to 3 pm, in CTLM 246L, and bring the exact output. And if anything on this page seems wrong or unclear, tell me; that is a bug too, and I would rather fix it than have you guess.
