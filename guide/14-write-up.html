Part III · The ladder

The inventory and EVAL.md

The component inventory says, for every skill and subagent you built, why it exists and what it bought. EVAL.md sets your reviews beside the numbers and says what each step bought and what it cost. This page goes through both, field by field and section by section.

About 42 minutes to read.

The short path

Why

These files are where your claims meet your evidence: each component's purpose and its with-and-without, each step's gain against its price, and, in your open review of the test topics, whether the gains held up where you never tuned. I read the inventory and EVAL.md for one thing above all, whether each claim matches its evidence, and together they are 25 of the 150 points.

Do
  1. In VS Code's editor, once both runs on a test topic are in: your open review of that topic, under ## Test topics in reviews/final.md (The open review of each topic says what goes there).
  2. In VS Code's editor, finish reviews/components.md: your Purpose, Review and Kept in final for every skill and subagent, under the factual fields the Builder filled in.
  3. In a plain terminal, run the p3 report command that each section of EVAL.md names, paste its tables, and write your own prose under each section in the editor; uv run p3 check, there or through the Guide, then lists what is left in all three files.
Done when

uv run p3 check prints OK reviews/final.md, test topics: a complete review of each (...), OK EVAL.md is written and OK reviews/components.md: a complete entry for every skill and subagent in your snapshots (...), its components built line is OK, and uv run p3 next lists 11 The write-up under Done.

Then

Hand in: the video, the last check and the Canvas post.

The component inventory

reviews/components.md has one entry for every skill and every subagent you built anywhere on the ladder, from c1-skills to final, kept or dropped. A component is a skill (a folder under tutor/.claude/skills/ that holds a SKILL.md) or a subagent (a Markdown file under tutor/.claude/agents/ whose frontmatter gives it a name). Hooks, settings, tutor/CLAUDE.md and tutor/PREFERENCES.md are not components and get no entry.

The inventory replaces a separate design document. Why each component exists is asked at the component, next to its evidence, and EVAL.md holds the diagram of final as a whole. It is worth 10 of the 150 points: a purpose and a with-and-without review for every skill and subagent, with the minimums met.

An entry, field by field

The template ships one empty entry at the bottom of reviews/components.md, and an example of a finished entry inside an HTML comment above it. Replace the empty entry with your first component, and copy it for each one after that. This is the empty entry:

### TODO: the component's name

- Kind: TODO
- Step added: TODO
- Purpose: TODO
- With and without: TODO
- Review: TODO
- Kept in final: TODO

And this is the finished example from the template's comment, for a quiz reviewer added in c2-review:

### quiz-reviewer

- Kind: subagent
- Step added: c2-review
- Purpose: My c1 review found two quiz items that could be read two ways (q2 and q4) and one whose correct option repeated the stem; a reviewer with a fresh context reads the draft the way a learner will, which the quiz skill writing its own items cannot do, so it is a subagent and not a step in the skill.
- With and without: reviews/c2-review.md, Round 1 (without) and Round 2 (with)
- Review: Without it, q2 asked which design "is better" with no criterion; with it, every stem names its criterion and the reviewer's record shows two items flagged and fixed in one pass, for about 0.6M more tokens per quiz, which I think a quiz is worth, because a misread item teaches the wrong thing.
- Kept in final: yes

A field may run over several lines; it ends where the next field's label starts. This is what each one holds.

The heading, ### <name>

The component's name: the skill's folder name, such as quiz, or the subagent's name from its frontmatter, such as quiz-reviewer. A subagent's file name does not have to match its name, so take the name from the frontmatter. p3 check matches the heading to the components in your snapshots, ignoring case and spacing.

- Kind:

skill or subagent. The Builder may fill it in; p3 check compares it with your snapshots and says so when they disagree, as in "its entry says Kind skill, but configs/c2-review/ holds it as a subagent".

- Step added:

The step the component arrived in, by three rules:

  • Normally, the first of your snapshots that holds it: c1-skills, c2-review, c3-split or final.
  • For teach, c1-skills. It ships with the baseline, but you rewrite it in step 1, and the baseline is not one of your snapshots.
  • For a component you built in a round and took out again before the step's snapshot, the step whose round built it.

The Builder may fill it in, and you check it. p3 check catches a step that does not match your snapshots, as in "quiz: Step added is c2-review, but it first appears in configs/c1-skills/".

- Purpose:

Yours. Why the tutor needs this component, and why it is a skill or a subagent rather than the other. The best purpose names what led to it: a finding of your review, with its round or criterion, as the example does with q2 and q4.

For the second half, it helps to remember what each kind is. A skill is instructions the main conversation loads and follows, so it works with everything that conversation already has: the learner's request, the chat, the files already read. For the four job skills the bench settles part of the answer, because it starts each job with /teach, /tutor, /quiz or /grade; say what the skill does in the main conversation that a subagent could not, such as the tutor answering in the chat, where the reply that gets scored is the session's final message. A subagent is a separate Claude with its own system prompt, its own tools and a fresh context window, and it sees only the task message it is given and what it reads for itself. That is why a reviewer is usually a subagent: it reads the draft the way the learner will, without the writer's reasoning to lean on. A subagent also costs more, because every subagent pays a share of Claude Code's fixed load of instructions and tools again, plus whatever it reads.

- With and without:

Where the evidence is. Usually that is the quick round that added the component: its Run line is the tutor without it, and the next round's Run line is the tutor with it, as in reviews/c2-review.md, Round 1 (without) and Round 2 (with). The Builder may fill it in, and you check it. A few cases need care:

  • Added in the last round of a step. There is no next round, so the "with" is the step's full run, which its major review reviews, as in reviews/c1-skills.md, Round 4 (without) and the major review of c1-skills (with).
  • teach. The round where you rewrote it, with the baseline's lesson as the output without it.
  • Taken out again before a snapshot. The rounds that added it and removed it.
  • Two components added in one round. A separate with-and-without check of each, and where it is, since the round alone cannot tell them apart.
- Review:

Yours. Your short review of the output with the component against the output without it, with evidence: a quote, an item id, a number that moved, and what it cost. The example says what was wrong without it (q2's missing criterion), what changed with it (every stem names its criterion; two items flagged and fixed), the price (about 0.6M more tokens per quiz), and the judgment (worth it, and why). If you saw no difference, say so; that is a result.

Live runs are not committed, so when a with-and-without rests on two live runs, copy the numbers you want to cite, such as tokens from the run's run.json, into your round's thoughts when you see them.

- Kept in final:

Yours. yes, or no, because and the reason, such as what the evidence showed. It must start with yes or no, and a no needs its reason. Once configs/final/ exists, p3 check compares this field with it, as in "Kept in final says yes, but configs/final/ does not hold it", so write it when you snapshot final.

Which fields the Builder fills, and which are yours

Who writes each field of an inventory entry, from reviews/README.md and the root CLAUDE.md.
FieldWho writes it
the heading, Kind, Step added, With and withoutthe Builder may fill these in, when it acts on a round that adds or changes a component; you check them
Purpose, Review, Kept in finalyou

When the Builder acts on a round that adds or changes a component, it adds or updates that component's entry with the factual fields only, and leaves Purpose, Review and Kept in final as markers for you. When a round removes a component, it leaves the entry in place for you to complete. It never writes or edits your three fields, even when asked to "finish the inventory".

If an entry is missing, for example for a component built in a session where the Builder did not add one, ask the Builder for it:

Say to the Builder
Add an entry for the tutor skill to reviews/components.md, with its factual fields only.

At the end of a step, when entries are missing, p3 next gives you the words for all of them at once: "Add an entry to reviews/components.md for each component of configs/<step>/ that has none (...), with Kind, Step added, and With and without."

Write Purpose when the component arrives, because you know then why you wanted it, and Review once the next round's run shows the tutor with it. p3 next asks, at the end of each step, for an entry for each component of the step's snapshot with Purpose and Review written.

Dropped components stay

Every component you built keeps its entry, whether final kept it or not, because a component that did not pay is evidence too.

  • A component final dropped, such as a reviewer from c2-review you left out of final, keeps its entry with Kept in final: no, because and the reason. It still counts toward the minimums, because a snapshot holds it.
  • A component you built in a round and took out before any snapshot gets an entry too. Its Step added is the step whose round built it, its With and without names the rounds that added and removed it, and its Kept in final is no, because and what the next round showed. p3 check cannot see such a component, because no snapshot holds it, so keeping its entry is up to you, and it does not count toward the minimums.

The second kind is often your best example of a change that did not pay, which EVAL.md section 11 asks for.

Example: an entry for a component taken out again, made up, in a student's words 
### references-finder

- Kind: subagent
- Step added: c3-split
- Purpose: My c2 lesson had two references, both to the same tutorial; a subagent with web search and a fresh context could look for sources while the writer drafts, which a step in the teach skill would do in the same long context.
- With and without: reviews/c3-split.md, Round 2 (without; this round added it) and Round 3 (with; this round removed it)
- Review: With it the lesson had six links instead of two, but two of the six were dead and three pointed at pages far above a novice; the teach scenario took about 1.4M more tokens. Not worth it for this learner.
- Kept in final: no, because Round 3's run showed more links but not better ones, at a real cost.

The minimums

The minimums are counted over what you built across the ladder, not over what final keeps, so dropping a component for a good reason never costs you.

The minimums, from README.md. The four skills' names are fixed so the bench can start each job with /teach, /tutor, /quiz or /grade.
SectionSkillsSubagents
498Eat least the four: teach, tutor, quiz and gradeat least five, for example three reviewers in c2-review and two roles in the c3-split pipeline
598Eat least six: the four and two of your ownat least eight
  • A component counts when it appears in one of your snapshots: any folder under configs/ other than c0-baseline, which includes final-minus-<x> and any snapshot of your own.
  • A component taken out before any snapshot keeps its entry but does not count.
  • A Markdown file under .claude/agents/ counts as a subagent only when it has frontmatter with a name, opening on its first line, which is also what Claude Code needs to load it. A checklist file that a subagent reads is fine there; p3 check notes it as documentation with a warning and does not count it.
  • p3 check reads your section from p3.json, so make sure section says "498E" or "598E".

What p3 check checks

uv run p3 check compares the inventory with the skills and subagents in your snapshots. It lists:

  • every component in a snapshot (other than c0-baseline) that has no entry, or whose entry is not complete;
  • a Kind, a Step added or a Kept in final that your snapshots contradict;
  • two entries for the same component;
  • the template's empty entry, if you leave it: "fill it in for one component, or delete it";
  • and, on a line of its own, how many skills and subagents your snapshots hold against your section's minimums.

With a first snapshot of c1-skills that still has no tutor skill, and two slips in the quiz entry, its inventory lines look like this:

Run in the Builder, or a plain terminal
uv run p3 check

          grade (skill, in configs/c1-skills/): no entry; add ### grade with its six fields
          quiz: Purpose is still TODO
          quiz: Review is still TODO
          quiz: Kept in final is 'maybe'; start it with yes or no (no, because ...)
          quiz: Step added is c2-review, but it first appears in configs/c1-skills/
          teach (skill, in configs/c1-skills/): no entry; add ### teach with its six fields

          skills: grade, quiz, teach

Every ### heading in components.md that holds one of the six field lines is an entry. A ### section of your own notes with none of them is fine. Save the file as UTF-8, as VS Code does by default; Your reviews says how to fix a file that a command saved as UTF-16.

The guard has no entry

The guard you add in final is a hook, not a skill or a subagent, so it has no entry, and neither does the script it runs. EVAL.md section 7 covers it, including why it is a hook. final: the guard, repeats and the ablation is where you build it.

EVAL.md, section by section

EVAL.md is the report on what each step of your architecture bought and what it cost. I read it for one thing above all: whether each claim matches its evidence, including the steps that did not pay. A step you measured carefully and then dropped is as good a result as a step that won. It is worth 15 of the 150 points: the trade-offs, the judge check, your open reviews against your blind picks, and the change that did not pay.

Three kinds of material go in it.

  • Tables pasted from commands: uv run p3 report for the objective checks, the cost and your ratings, and uv run p3 compare --reveal <session-id> for each blind comparison. Paste them as they are, and name the run ids or the session id next to each one, so I can find them in bench/runs/ and bench/compare/.
  • Pointers into reviews/: name the round, such as c2-review Round 3, or the criterion, such as the c1-skills review's Wording and possible misreadings, rather than retelling it. The trail from thoughts to change to outcome is already in reviews/.
  • Your prose. It is yours alone: the Builder never writes it, and the Guide can explain what a section asks but never drafts an answer. If you want help with wording, ask in a chat and type your own version.

EVAL.md starts with a short list, "Words used below", that defines Your ratings, a round, a pass, a tally, the p-value, could tell and cost; read it once before you start. Each section ends with a marker line that starts with TODO. Replace each one with your writing.

Watch out

p3 check counts every line of EVAL.md that holds the word TODO, wherever it is, even inside a table or a code block you pasted. That is stricter than in reviews/, where a TODO in code is a quote. So leave no TODO anywhere in EVAL.md; until then p3 check prints a line such as TODO EVAL.md: 30 TODO markers left (lines 31, 38, 49, ...), and once they are gone, OK EVAL.md is written.

How to paste a table

Run the command in a plain terminal, select its output from the first ## line to the end, and paste it into EVAL.md in the editor, at the section's marker. The tables are Markdown, so they show as tables on GitHub. p3 report makes no model call, so it costs nothing to run again.

1. The baseline, c0-baseline

When: week 1, once the baseline's three full runs are scored.

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 report --configs c0-baseline

Paste the table and name the three run ids, which are the folder names of the full runs under bench/runs/c0-baseline/ (your quick run from setup is there too, and the report leaves it out). Then answer what the section asks: what the baseline did well and where it fell short, for each of the four jobs; which of the gaps in KNOWN-GAPS.md you saw in your own runs, and whether you found one that is not listed; and which number moved the most from one run to the next.

How far the numbers go: this section sets your yardstick. The baseline's ranges are the only measure of run-to-run spread you have until final's repeats, and steps 1 to 3 are measured on one run each, so say what that spread means for a step measured once. Cite your runs, not mine; KNOWN-GAPS.md is what I saw.

2. Step 1: c1-skills against c0-baseline

When: at the end of step 1, after its major review; p3 next asks for this section with no TODO before it moves you to step 2.

What changed. Name the rounds that built the step, such as c1-skills Rounds 1 to 4, and the inventory entries of the skills they added, without retelling them. Then what you put in PREFERENCES.md, and anything about how the four skills work together that no single entry says.

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 report --configs c0-baseline,c1-skills

What the step bought and what it cost. For each job, what your major review found against c0-baseline, and whether the objective checks agree; where they disagree, which you believe and why; what the step cost per scenario; and whether the gain was worth it.

How far the numbers go: one run of c1-skills against three of c0-baseline. A c1-skills value inside the baseline's range is not a difference you can claim, and one outside it is a hint, not a result, because you do not know c1-skills's own spread. And c1-skills is the first config with your PREFERENCES.md, while c0-baseline keeps the neutral one, so this step measures your skills and your preferences together. Say so, and say which differences you think came from which, and why.

3. Step 2: c2-review against c1-skills

When: at the end of step 2, after its major review.

What changed. The rounds and the reviewers' inventory entries, as in step 1. Then the design of each loop, for teach, quiz and grade: who reviews; what is on each reviewer's checklist, and which items came from your c1-skills major review; how each reviewer gets a fresh context; how many passes a loop may run and what ends it early; and what each loop records. (A pass is one revise-and-review iteration inside a loop, never a quick round.) Then one design choice you could have made the other way, such as one reviewer against several, a cap of one pass against three, or a reviewer that edits against one that only reports, and why you chose as you did.

The loops as they ran. For teach, quiz and grade: how many passes ran in your bench run, what the reviewers flagged, and whether the revisions moved anything you can point at. The loops' records in the run's outputs are the evidence; name the files, such as quiz/outputs/review-records/NNNN-quiz.md in the run's folder if you used the folder step 2 suggests.

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 report --configs c1-skills,c2-review

What the step bought and what it cost. Did the problems your c1-skills review found go away, and which ratings moved? Which loop earned its cost, and which did not? If a loop ran several passes and the final output was no better than the first draft, say so, because that is the finding.

How far the numbers go: in my baseline runs, the objective checks for the quiz and the grades were already at their best, so on the shared topic they act as floors that catch a step that breaks something. A review loop on quiz or grade has little room to raise them, and what it buys shows up in your own reviews and in its cost. Say so plainly if that is what you find; it is a real result.

4. Step 3: c3-split against c2-review

When: at the end of step 3, after its major review.

What changed. The rounds and the pipeline's inventory entries. Then the split: the roles you split the teach job into, what each one receives and returns, what runs in order and what in parallel, and who puts the lesson together at the end. Why this split and not a smaller one, and what you tried and took out again.

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 report --configs c2-review,c3-split

What the step bought and what it cost. What the split changed in the lesson (its structure, its figures, its references, its fit to you) and how your four Lesson ratings moved against c2-review's. What it cost in tokens, minutes and subagents against c2-review, and how much of that was overhead rather than writing. The teach scenario's entry in the run's run.json gives its tokens, turns and subagents, and its trace, teach/trace.jsonl.gz, holds every subagent's messages, if you want to see where the work went.

How far the numbers go: each step has one run, so say how confident you are, and what would change your mind.

5. final against c0-baseline, with repeats

When: week 3, once final has its three scored full runs.

What went into final. First the diagram of final; The diagram of final below says what goes in it. Then what you kept from each step, what you dropped, and why, pointing at the inventory entries rather than repeating them. Then, if final had to get cheaper tomorrow, which component you would cut first and which last, and what you expect the tutor to lose.

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 report --configs c0-baseline,final

What the repeats let you claim. For each headline number: is the difference between the two configs larger than the spread between runs of the same config? Where the ranges overlap, write "not distinguishable with three runs", and do not rank the two by their means. Then the overall cost: how many times more tokens and minutes per scenario final takes, and for which jobs it is worth it, by your review and by the numbers.

How far the numbers go: three runs of each, the most repeats the project asks for; Reading repeats walks through an example. Like step 1, this comparison measures your skills and your preferences together, because c0-baseline keeps the neutral PREFERENCES.md and final has yours.

6. The ablation

When: after the full run of your final-minus-<x> snapshot, which is final with one component removed. final: the guard, repeats and the ablation says how to make it.

The section asks what you expected to happen before you ran it. A prediction written after you saw the result is not a prediction, so write it into this section, and commit it, before you start the ablation's run.

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 report --configs final,final-minus-<x>

Use your ablation's real name in place of final-minus-<x>, such as final-minus-quiz-review. Then say what changed, in quality and in cost, and what you saw in its outputs that the numbers do not show.

How far the numbers go: one run against three. If the ablation's value sits inside final's range, one run cannot say the component matters; if it sits well outside, that is a hint worth stating as a hint.

7. The guard

When: week 3, once final has run and you have a trace that shows the guard at work in the bench. final: the guard, repeats and the ablation is where you build and test it; this section is where you report it.

It asks for:

  • what the guard blocks and what it allows, and how it tells the grader from the main conversation;
  • why it is a PreToolUse hook in tutor/.claude/settings.json, and not a permission deny rule, a hook in a subagent's frontmatter, a subagent's tools list, or an instruction in a skill;
  • what changed elsewhere in the tutor because of it, such as grading handed to a subagent the guard allows;
  • the result of running its tests, pasted, and the cases they cover: the main conversation reading keys/ with each of Read, Grep, Glob, Bash and PowerShell, the grader reading them, and the reads that must still work;
  • the bench evidence: the run id and scenario of a trace where the hook allowed the grader's read of keys/ or blocked the main conversation's, since a hook that fails to start lets every call through (or, when its entry sets "onFailure": "block", blocks every call, the grader's too; final: the guard, repeats and the ablation explains);
  • tutor_leaks for final against c0-baseline, and any denial of the guard you found in a trace;
  • the gap you know of: one way the main conversation could still reach a key, or a case your tests do not cover, and why you accepted it or how you closed it.
Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
python -m unittest discover -s tests -t .

That runs every test in tests/, the course's and yours, and none of them calls Claude; on macOS and Linux, use python3. To run only your guard's file, name it, as in python -m unittest tests.test_guard.

Reading repeats

Lecture 23 (Tue Nov 17) is about repeats with intervals, and my pace for week 3 (A pace that works) asks you to read your repeats the way it does. Until then, and as the floor for every comparison in EVAL.md, the rule is the one section 5 states: compare the difference between two configs with the spread within each config.

What a cell says. 0.44 (0.33-0.50) is the mean over a config's scored full runs, then the lowest and the highest run in brackets. A cell with no brackets is a single run, or runs that all gave the same value; the runs column says which. n=2 in the brackets means only two of the runs could be scored on that check, and a dash means none could.

An example, made up, on the scale my baseline showed. In my three baseline runs, tutor_behavior_met was 0.33 to 0.50. Suppose your report shows:

A made-up behavior met column, with three runs of each config, and what each reading lets you claim.
Configrunsbehavior metWhat you can claim
c0-baseline30.44 (0.33-0.50)the yardstick
final, first case30.61 (0.50-0.67)the ranges meet at 0.50: not distinguishable with three runs, though the mean is higher
final, second case30.78 (0.67-0.83)every final run is above every baseline run: a difference you can state, for these runs on this topic

Two more things to keep in mind when you read a share.

  • Shares on a few items move in big steps. tutor_behavior_met is a share of six probes, so a run can only score 0, 0.17, 0.33, 0.50, 0.67, 0.83 or 1, and one probe changing its verdict moves it by 0.17. The same is true of a six-item quiz. A difference of 0.17 between two single runs can be one reply.
  • A single run has no spread of its own. Steps 1 to 3 and the ablation are measured once. Set each single run against the range of the config it is compared with where that config has repeats: c0-baseline's for c1-skills, final's for the ablation. For c2-review against c1-skills and c3-split against c2-review, one run against one, the baseline's and final's ranges are the best guide you have to how much a number moves by chance.

Read the cost tables the same way: tokens and minutes vary from run to run too, and the tables show their ranges. If you use an interval from lecture 23, say how you computed it.

The judge check

The judge is a fixed prompt in bench/judge/ and a pinned model (claude-sonnet-5-5), used only for what a script cannot check: whether the lesson's key facts are present and correct, whether it asserts a listed misconception, its fit to the learner's level, and whether a tutor reply did what the probe expected. Several of your headline numbers rest on it, and a judge you have not checked is a number you cannot lean on. So after lecture 24 you check it against ten labels of your own, the way lecture 24 checks a model judge, and report how often you agree. The labels are yours to make; no agent makes them for you.

  1. Choose ten decisions by a rule, before you look at any verdict. For example, all six tutor probes of one run of final and the first four of one run of c0-baseline, or ten facts from two lessons, since the judge checks the six facts of the teach objective in each. Choosing by a rule keeps you from picking the cases you already feel sure about.

  2. Read what the judge was asked, and what it judged. For a tutor reply: the probe's message and its expected behavior in bench/topics/agent-architecture/probes.json, the reply in the run's tutor-pNN/result.json, and the judge's prompt in bench/judge/tutor.md. For a fact: the teach objective's facts in bench/topics/agent-architecture/topic.json (the ones whose objective is O3), and the lesson in teach/outputs/lessons/.

  3. Write down your label first, in EVAL.md section 10: met or not met for a reply, present and correct or not for a fact.

  4. Then read the judge's verdict in that run's score.json, which holds every check's result: for each tutor probe, the judge's met or not met and its notes; for the lesson, its verdict on each fact. Write it beside your label.

  5. Count and read the disagreements. How many of ten did you agree on? For each disagreement: is the judge wrong, are you, or is the expected behavior unclear? Then say what that agreement means for the judge numbers you reported in the sections above.

Say which numbers your check covers. Ten tutor labels tell you about tutor_behavior_met, and nothing directly about teach_facts_correct; if your argument leans on one judge number more than the others, check that one.
