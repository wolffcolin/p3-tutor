Part III · The ladder

Step 2: review loops (c2-review)

In this step you add a review loop to each of the teach, quiz and grade jobs: a reviewer subagent with a checklist reads the draft, the skill revises it, and the loop stops at a cap you set. Then you measure what the reviews bought and what they cost, against c1-skills.

About 28 minutes to read.

The short path

Why

A reviewer subagent reads a draft with a fresh context and a specific checklist, the way the learner will, and this step finds out whether that second reading makes the outputs better. It is never free: in my pilot, review loops made the teach scenario about 6.7 times as expensive in tokens.

Do
  1. Add one reviewer per quick round, so at least three rounds: run the live tutor in the Builder or a plain terminal, write your thoughts under the round in reviews/c2-review.md in the VS Code editor, then say to the Builder "Act on Round k of reviews/c2-review.md.", with k the round's number.
  2. When teach, quiz and grade each have a loop, snapshot it in the Builder or a plain terminal with uv run p3 snapshot c2-review, and start its full run in a Plain terminal with uv run p3 bench --config c2-review.
  3. In the editor, write the major review (lesson, quiz and feedback) in reviews/c2-review.md, section 3 of EVAL.md, and the Purpose and Review of each reviewer in reviews/components.md; then commit and push, in a Plain terminal or through the Builder.
Done when

uv run p3 next lists 5 Step 2, c2-review under Done, and its Now line has moved on: to 7 Step 3, c3-split, or, for 598E and the length study, to 6 The length study, part 1.

Then

Step 3: split the teach job (c3-split), or for the length study, PREREG.md before the runs. Whenever you are unsure what comes next, ask the Guide, or run uv run p3 next in a plain terminal.

What a review loop is

A review loop has four parts:

  1. A writer. The job's skill, in the main conversation, writes a draft into a folder of drafts.
  2. A reviewer. A subagent, which is a separate Claude with its own instructions, its own tools and a fresh context, reads the draft against a checklist and returns its findings.
  3. A revision. The skill fixes what the reviewer found.
  4. A cap. The loop runs at most a number of revise-and-review passes that you choose, and stops early when the reviewer finds nothing left to fix. Then the skill writes the final file where the bench reads it.

I call each revise-and-review iteration a pass, so it is never confused with your quick rounds in reviews/. A loop capped at two passes reviews a draft at most twice.

Why it might help

A skill that checks its own draft in the same conversation reads it with its own reasoning still in view. A subagent starts with a context of its own and sees only what you hand it, so it reads the draft the way the learner will, without the writer's reasoning to lean on. Give it a checklist built from what you found wrong in c1-skills, and it looks for exactly those problems.

What it costs

Every claude -p session carries a fixed load before it does any work, 24,000 to 49,000 input tokens in my checks, almost all of them cached reads of Claude Code's own instructions and tools. Every subagent pays a share of that again, plus whatever it reads, such as the whole draft. And every pass adds time. These are my pilot's numbers, from Claude Code 2.1.314 with Sonnet: the baseline averaged over three full runs, against one full run of a review-loop config of my own, with four skills and reviewers on teach, quiz and grade, at most two passes each.

Where the gain can show

In my baseline runs the quiz keys agreed with both solvers on every item, and the grades matched every true verdict (KNOWN-GAPS.md). On the shared topic those objective checks act as floors: they catch a step that breaks something, and a loop on quiz or grade has little room to raise them. What a loop buys shows up in your own reviews of its output, such as difficulty, coverage, wording, and feedback that explains and fits you, and in its cost. If that is what you find, say so plainly; it is a real result.

Quality, not agreement

A review here is about quality, not agreement, and a reviewer that votes "looks good" is worth nothing: it costs a subagent and changes nothing. "Review this" gets you a polite summary; "check that every distractor matches a named misconception and that no stem gives away its answer" gets you a finding. Four habits make a reviewer find things:

  • A specific checklist, one check per line, each one something a reader could answer yes or no from the draft.
  • Findings with evidence: for each problem, the item id, section or quote, what is wrong, and what would fix it.
  • A word on every check, a finding or "none", so a check the reviewer skipped is visible rather than silent.
  • A clear job for the writer: fix each finding, or say in the record why not.

An example of a quiz reviewer's checklist, started from a c1 review:

1. Every stem names the criterion it asks about. A stem that asks for the "best" or "better" option without saying for what is a finding (my c1 review, q2).
2. No correct option repeats a phrase from its stem that no wrong option repeats.
3. Every wrong option matches a misconception the key names, by its id.
4. Each item has one defensible answer: answer it from the stem alone, and report any item where a second option could also be argued.
5. The items test the core of the objective, not side details.

What each job's reviews check

Add review loops on teach, quiz and grade, with the reviewers as subagents; the tutor gets none. These are the dimensions I expect each job's reviews to cover, and they are the same criteria as your own major reviews (Your reviews has them in full):

What each job's reviews check: the same criteria as your major reviews.
JobWhat the review checks
Quizdifficulty; coverage of the lesson's core; wording and the ways an item could be misunderstood; writing quality; alignment with the learning goal
Gradewhether the feedback is useful, explains enough, and is personalized the way this learner likes
Teachseparate reviews for technical correctness and fact-checking, for pedagogy, for alignment with the learning goal, and for overall quality

The grade reviewer judges feedback "the way this learner likes", so it has to read PREFERENCES.md; say so in its instructions or its task message, because a subagent sees only what it is handed or told to read.

Teach: four separate reviews, built one of two ways

On teach, "separate" means that each of the four reviews (correctness and facts, pedagogy, alignment, overall quality) gets its own checklist and its own findings, so a fact-check is never folded into a general impression. How you build that is yours:

Whichever you choose, the fact-check checks claims against sources, not against the reviewer's own impression. Give that reviewer WebSearch and WebFetch, which the shipped tutor/.claude/settings.json already allows, and ask it to name the source for each correction it asks for.

One reviewer per job, three in c2-review, is what the 498E example counts toward the minimum of five subagents across the ladder; four teach reviewers give you six. 598E needs at least eight subagents in all, so the four-reviewer build may suit you; Stretch options and the 598E length study has the details.

Advice that will save you time

Start each checklist from your c1-skills review

Each problem your c1-skills major review found, with its item id or quote, is a checklist item, and your c2-review major review then shows whether the reviewers caught it. The Builder reads your c1 review when it builds a reviewer and turns its findings into the checklist, so the more specific that review was, the better the checklist it gets.

Write each check as a rule a reviewer can apply on any topic, with your c1 finding as its example, as in "every stem names its criterion (my c1 review, q2)". Never copy text from the shared topic's files into a checklist: a config that treats a bench item specially gets no credit on that topic, and p3 check warns when a config file holds 40 or more characters copied from the shared topic. Your test topics will show whether the checklist works where you never tuned it.

Give each reviewer a fresh context, and hand it what it needs

A subagent does not see the main conversation's history, the skills it invoked or the files it read. It starts with its own instructions (the body of its file), the task message the main conversation writes for it, the CLAUDE.md files (in the bench, only the tutor's), and any skills its skills field names. So the task message has to name the draft's path and anything else to read, such as PREFERENCES.md or MISSION.md, and the checklist belongs in the reviewer's own file or a file it is told to read. Do not pass it the writer's reasoning: the point is a reading without it. tutor/.claude/agents/README.md explains what a subagent sees, and Your tutor, configs and snapshots explains skills and subagents.

Cap the passes

Decide how many revise-and-review passes a loop may run, and what ends it early, such as a review with no finding. A loop with no cap is a loop that can spend your week, and in the bench it can also run into the scenario's time or turn limit, which turns it into a recorded timeout. The limits are set in bench/scenarios.json and are the same at every lesson length:

The limits of each scenario, from the README.
ScenarioTimeTurns
teach45 minutes200
quiz, each grade15 minutes80
each tutor probe10 minutes40

My own config capped every loop at two passes, and its teach scenario took 9.8 minutes. Design yours to finish well inside the limits.

Tell the skill to wait for the reviewer's report

A subagent the main conversation starts runs in the background by default. In a claude -p session, which is how the bench runs every scenario, Claude waits for it when it judges it should or when it is told to. So the skill should say plainly to wait for the reviewer's report before it revises or finishes; otherwise the skill may finish before the review comes back.

Give each reviewer an explicit tools list

A subagent whose file leaves out tools gets every tool available to subagents, the Agent tool included, so it can start subagents of its own. A reviewer that only reports needs little more than Read, Glob, Grep, plus WebSearch and WebFetch for a fact-check. Whether a reviewer edits the draft or only reports on it is a design choice, and EVAL.md asks you about one choice you could have made the other way; a reviewer that only reports keeps every change in the skill, where the record can list it.

Know what the quiz review checks coverage against

In the bench, the quiz scenario's workspace has no lesson, so there "coverage of the lesson's core" means the objective's text and what MISSION.md and NOTES.md say the learner needs. In your own sessions, the quiz reviewer should check a quiz against the lesson in lessons/ when there is one. Write the reviewer so it handles both: the lesson when it exists, the objective and the learner files when it does not.

Record what happened

Have each loop write a short record in a folder of the tutor's own, for example review-records/NNNN-quiz.md: how many passes ran, what each reviewer flagged, and what changed. I suggest that name and not reviews/, so the tutor's records are never confused with your own reviews in the repo's reviews/. The bench keeps every new file a scenario writes, so the record lands in the run's outputs, such as quiz/outputs/review-records/, and you can then say whether the revisions moved anything, which section 3 of EVAL.md asks.

Keep drafts out of the folders the bench reads

The teach check expects exactly one new lesson in lessons/, so a draft saved there turns a good run into a recorded failure; the quiz check scores one new quiz and its key, and when it finds several new quizzes it warns and scores only the last by number. Put drafts in a folder of your own, such as drafts/, and write the final file once. The bench keeps the drafts too, so you can set the first draft beside the final file and see what the loop changed.

Plan for the guard in final now

In final you add a guard, a hook that keeps the answer keys in keys/ away from the main conversation. A quiz skill that reads or edits a key after writing it will then be blocked, so final needs it to work on a draft in drafts/ and write the key once, or to leave the key to a subagent the guard allows. A quiz loop that drafts in drafts/ and writes quizzes/ and keys/ once at the end already works that way. The guard explains it.

Watch the permission denials

In my own review-loop config, the teach session tried seven times to copy the finished draft into lessons/ with a shell command, was denied each time, and then wrote the whole lesson out again, which cost output tokens and turns. run.json lists every scenario's permission_denials. Either allow the narrow command you need in tutor/.claude/settings.json, or tell the skill how to finish without it, for example by writing the final file directly with its file-writing tool, which the bench allows inside the scenario's folder. That allow list also covers your subagents' tool calls, and the Agent tool that starts a subagent needs no entry.

Quick rounds: one reviewer per round

You build this step in quick rounds, as in step 1: you run the tutor, read what it made, write a few sentences of thoughts in reviews/c2-review.md, and the Builder makes one change. Your reviews explains quick rounds in general. Here the rule is one reviewer per round, so each reviewer's with-and-without is the run before and after its round. A round that adds a reviewer also changes the skill that calls it, because the loop lives in the skill; that is one change, of Kind new subagent.

One plan that works, with one lesson reviewer. The order is yours.
RoundThe run at its startThe change the Builder makes
Round 1the tutor as c1-skills left itnew subagent: a quiz reviewer, and the quiz skill's loop
Round 2--only quiz,grade-s1: the quiz with its reviewer; grading withoutnew subagent: a grade reviewer
Round 3--only grade-s1,teach: grading with its reviewer; the lesson withoutnew subagent: a lesson reviewer
The full runthe lesson with its reviewernone: this is the snapshot's run

Make each round's run cover two jobs, as the plan does: the one whose reviewer the last round added (its "with") and the one the next round changes (its "without"). The lesson reviewer's "with" is the step's full run, which your major review covers. With four teach reviewers, add one per round after Round 3, each round running --only teach.

For Round 1, if you have not changed tutor/ since you snapshotted c1-skills, its full run already is the output without your first reviewer, and your c1 major review has read it closely; you can name it on Round 1's Run line instead of making a new run.

Watch out

p3 check counts only two complete rounds, and neither it nor p3 next can tell whether teach, quiz and grade each have a loop. So from two complete rounds on, p3 next gives the snapshot with that condition in front: "When teach, quiz and grade each have their review loop and you are happy with the tutor, snapshot it: uv run p3 snapshot c2-review (or add another round first)." This step needs all three, and with one reviewer per round that is at least three rounds. Add rounds until they all have their loop.

One round, as an example

  1. Write your thoughts in reviews/c2-review.md, in the editor. Open the file in VS Code, find ### Round 1, and replace the markers on its Run line and under **My thoughts:**. Save it.

What p3 next says during the rounds

The same as in step 1, with reviews/c2-review.md. For an untouched round it asks you to run the live tutor and write what you noticed under Round k > My thoughts; once your thoughts are written, "In the Builder window, say: Act on Round k of reviews/c2-review.md."; for anything else missing, "Finish Round k in reviews/c2-review.md:" and its first problem; and with every round complete, either "Add Round k to reviews/c2-review.md by copying the last round with its marker lines." or, from two complete rounds on, "When teach, quiz and grade each have their review loop and you are happy with the tutor, snapshot it: uv run p3 snapshot c2-review (or add another round first)."

Snapshot

When teach, quiz and grade each have a loop, which with one reviewer per round takes at least three complete rounds, and you are happy with the tutor, try the whole loop once on a live teach scenario before you freeze it, because the teach loop is the expensive one and the one closest to its limit. Look at that scenario in run.json for a timeout, a turn limit or a run of denials. Then freeze it:

Run in the Builder, or a plain terminal
uv run p3 snapshot c2-review
configs/c2-review/: ... config files, sha256:...
Run it with: p3 bench --config c2-review

The snapshot copies tutor/CLAUDE.md, tutor/PREFERENCES.md and everything under tutor/.claude/, your reviewers' files in tutor/.claude/agents/ among them, into configs/c2-review/, with a hash in SNAPSHOT.json. Your learner files and tutor/.claude/settings.local.json are never part of it. It makes no model call. Page 05 explains snapshots in full.

Watch out

A snapshot is frozen once it has runs: a run's numbers have to belong to one exact tutor, and overwriting a snapshot that has runs makes p3 check fail, because those runs no longer match their config. Run uv run p3 check right after the snapshot: it should show config c2-review: the four skills, and if something is wrong, fix it and snapshot again with --force while there are still no runs.

Do your live tries for the major review right after the snapshot, while tutor/ still matches it, and before you start step 3.

The full run

A full run is the ten scenarios on the shared topic plus their scoring, and c2-review needs one scored full run, because it is the run your major review reviews and the one uv run p3 report sets beside c1-skills; quick and --only runs are left out of both. Check /usage in a Claude Code window first: my review-loop config took 6.99 million tokens and 17.1 minutes of scenario time per full run. Then start it in a plain terminal, a terminal tab with no Claude Code in it, in work/p3-tutor:

Run in a plain terminal in work/p3-tutor
uv run p3 bench --config c2-review

It belongs in a plain terminal because it can take longer than Claude Code lets a foreground command run, ten minutes at most, and it must not stop when you close a Claude Code window. It names the run folder, bench/runs/c2-review/<run-id>, as it starts, and prints a summary table and the scores when it finishes. While it runs, do your live tries (below), and the reviewers' inventory entries, which uv run p3 next usually names on its Also line after "While it runs".

If it fails

A usage limit or a sign-in problem stops the run, and that repeat is set aside under bench/runs/_live/_aborted/ because it measures nothing; wait for the reset and run it again. A scenario that times out is different: that is a result about your loop, so keep the run and report it. When it breaks covers both.

The major review

The major review of c2-review covers the lesson, the quiz and the feedback, criterion by criterion, each rated from 1 to 5 with written feedback and evidence. The tutor is not reviewed here, because it gets no review loop. Its previous step is c1-skills. Plan on about 1.75 hours. You write all of it, in the editor, under ## Major review in reviews/c2-review.md.

The Run line and the outputs

Replace the TODO in **Run:** bench/runs/c2-review/TODO with your run's id; p3 check accepts it only when it names a scored full run of c2-review on the shared topic. Then read that run's outputs for the three jobs: the lesson in teach/outputs/lessons/ in your browser (right-click it in VS Code, "Reveal in File Explorer" or "Reveal in Finder", then double-click), the quiz and key in quiz/outputs/ (take the quiz first), and the grades in grade-s1/outputs/grades/ and grade-s2/outputs/grades/. Read the loops' records and drafts beside them: they show what each reviewer flagged and what changed.

Live tries, with fork mode off

From this step on, your tutor waits for subagents, and in an interactive session it can do that only once fork mode is off; fork mode is on by default. Before your live tries, put this in tutor/.claude/settings.local.json, in the editor, then start claude again in tutor/:

{"env": {"CLAUDE_CODE_FORK_SUBAGENT": "0"}}

If the file already exists, because Claude Code wrote your tool approvals into it, add the "env" key beside what is there rather than replacing the file. This file is personal and never part of a config, which is the point: in claude -p, turning fork mode off makes nested subagents start in the background instead, so putting it in tutor/.claude/settings.json would change how your tutor runs in the bench. The last section of tutor/.claude/agents/README.md says the same.

Then open a terminal in work/p3-tutor/tutor, start claude Your tutor, and use it as a learner: a short lesson, a quiz on it, your answers graded. Write what you did and saw under **Live tries:**.

Say to your tutor
/quiz Give me a four-question quiz on the lesson you just wrote.

In your own sessions the quiz reviewer can check coverage against that lesson, which the bench's quiz scenario cannot.

EVAL.md, section 3

Section 3 of EVAL.md, headed ## 3. Step 2: `c2-review` against `c1-skills`, has four parts, each ending in a marker line you replace with your own writing.

  1. What changed. Name the rounds and the reviewers' inventory entries. Then the design of each loop, for teach, quiz and grade: who reviews; what is on each checklist and which items came from your c1-skills major review; how each reviewer gets a fresh context; how many passes a loop may run and what ends it early; and what each loop records. Then one design choice you could have made the other way, such as one reviewer against several, a cap of one pass against three, or a reviewer that edits against one that only reports, and why you chose as you did.

  2. The loops as they ran. For each job, how many passes ran in your full run, what the reviewers flagged, and whether the revisions moved anything you can point at: a fact corrected, an ambiguous item rewritten, feedback that changed. Name the record files in the run's outputs.

  3. Your review, the objective checks and the cost. Paste the tables as they are, with the run ids:

    Run in the Builder, or a plain terminal
    uv run p3 report --configs c1-skills,c2-review

  4. What the step bought and what it cost. Did the problems your c1 review found go away, and which ratings moved? Which loop earned its cost and which did not? If a loop ran several passes and the final output was no better than the first draft, say so, because that is the finding.

The prose is yours. The Guide can explain what a question is asking, and it will not write the answer.

The inventory: an entry for each reviewer

Each reviewer gets an entry in reviews/components.md, headed with the subagent's name from its frontmatter, such as ### quiz-reviewer, so that each reviewer's reason for existing sits next to its evidence and a reviewer that did not pay shows up as plainly as one that did. The Builder fills in Kind (subagent), Step added (c2-review) and With and without (the round that added it, such as reviews/c2-review.md, Round 1 (without) and Round 2 (with)); you check them. You write the Purpose (why the tutor needs it, and why it is a subagent rather than a step in the skill) and the Review (the output with it against without it, with evidence). Kept in final waits until final is frozen, and until then p3 check lists the entry as not complete, which is expected; p3 next needs only the Purpose and the Review now. If a reviewer of the snapshot has no entry yet, p3 next gives you the words for the Builder: "Add an entry to reviews/components.md for each component of configs/c2-review/ that has none (...), with Kind, Step added, and With and without." The inventory and EVAL.md explains the inventory in full.

The template's own example of a finished entry, from the comment in reviews/components.md:

### quiz-reviewer

- Kind: subagent
- Step added: c2-review
- Purpose: My c1 review found two quiz items that could be read two ways (q2 and q4) and one whose correct option repeated the stem; a reviewer with a fresh context reads the draft the way a learner will, which the quiz skill writing its own items cannot do, so it is a subagent and not a step in the skill.
- With and without: reviews/c2-review.md, Round 1 (without) and Round 2 (with)
